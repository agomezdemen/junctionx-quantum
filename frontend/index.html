            BRIEFING / WHY QUANTUMTRACE

            Today’s secrets. Tomorrow’s threat.

            Understanding your cryptography is the first step toward protecting it.

            THE QUANTUM CHALLENGE

            Confidential data can outlive the protection around it.

            A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break the public-key algorithms behind much of today’s digital trust: RSA, elliptic-curve cryptography, and Diffie–Hellman. Encrypted communications captured today could become readable years later, while the information is still sensitive.

            Post-quantum standards give organizations a path forward. But planning a transition is difficult when teams cannot see where legacy cryptography lives across their applications, networks, and cloud environments.

            QuantumTrace is designed to close that visibility gap: discover cryptographic assets, map their exposure to quantum threats, and bring the evidence together in a Cryptography Bill of Materials (CBOM). That inventory is the starting point for deciding what to change and where to begin.

            UNDERSTANDING THE THREAT

            Two algorithms. Different consequences.

            Shor’s and Grover’s algorithms are quantum computing methods that challenge different parts of cryptography.

            01 / PUBLIC-KEY CRYPTOGRAPHY

            Shor’s algorithm

            Shor’s algorithm can efficiently factor large integers and solve discrete logarithms on a sufficiently capable quantum computer. These are the mathematical problems that protect RSA, Diffie–Hellman, and elliptic-curve systems such as ECDH and ECDSA.

            Why it mattersAn attacker could recover private keys, undermine vulnerable key exchanges, or forge digital signatures. This threatens both the confidentiality of communications and the identities we trust online.

            Increasing RSA or elliptic-curve key sizes does not remove this vulnerability. These uses need a transition to quantum-resistant alternatives.

            02 / SYMMETRIC KEYS & HASHES

            Grover’s algorithm

            Grover’s algorithm speeds up an unstructured search. In an idealized model, searching N possibilities takes roughly √N quantum queries. That can reduce the work needed to search for a symmetric encryption key or find an input matching a given hash.

            Why it mattersIt reduces security margins rather than breaking these algorithms in the same way as Shor’s. Key length, hash output length, and the cost of running the attack matter.

            The speedup is theoretical; practical quantum attacks face substantial overhead. NIST expects AES-192 and AES-256 to remain secure for a long time.

            These threats depend on capable quantum hardware; they do not mean today’s quantum computers can decrypt every system. The impact also depends on how an algorithm is used. Shor and Grover explained ↗ · NIST on symmetric-key security ↗

            SAVE NOW, DECODE LATER

            Harvest now, decrypt later

            An attacker may not need to read your data today to put its future confidentiality at risk.

            1. 01

              Collect today

              An adversary captures encrypted communications, even when they cannot decrypt them.

            2. 02

              Store for years

              The encrypted copies are retained while the information remains valuable.

            3. 03

              Decrypt in the future

              If a capable quantum computer becomes available, vulnerable cryptography could allow those copies to be read.

            Long-lived secrets make preparation relevant today. Updating a system later cannot retroactively protect copies already captured under vulnerable cryptography. NIST: why preparation matters ↗

            Prioritize the next step

            Use risk distributions and algorithm counts to identify findings for review. Combine these with data sensitivity and retention needs when planning changes.

            NIST’s migration project emphasizes discovering cryptographic dependencies and testing replacements. QuantumTrace supports that visibility; its report ratings are not a security certification. Explore NIST’s migration project ↗

            What comes after discovery?

            A planned transition to post-quantum cryptography

            Post-quantum algorithms are designed to resist attacks from both conventional and quantum computers. NIST finalized its first three standards in 2024: ML-KEM for key establishment, and ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for digital signatures. Migration also needs compatibility testing and coordination with the systems and vendors you rely on.
